## "Who Is Responsible When an Algorithm Discriminates?"

Automated decision-making systems are increasingly used to determine who gets a loan, who is called for a job interview, and even who is flagged as a potential criminal suspect. Proponents argue that algorithms are more objective than humans: they apply the same criteria consistently and are not subject to mood or personal bias. This makes them fairer, the argument goes, and therefore preferable to human judgment.

However, this view is mistaken. Algorithms are trained on historical data, and if that data reflects past discrimination — for example, if certain neighbourhoods were historically denied loans — the algorithm will reproduce and even amplify those patterns. The system is not neutral; it encodes the biases of the world that produced it.

Some respond that the solution is simply to improve the data. But this misses a deeper problem: the choice of what to optimise for is itself a value judgment. When a hiring algorithm is told to find candidates "similar to our best performers," it assumes that past success is the right model for the future — which may exclude people who would have thrived under different conditions.

For these reasons, algorithmic decision-making in high-stakes domains should not be treated as a technical matter alone. It requires democratic oversight, transparent criteria, and the right to human review. Technology companies cannot be left to regulate themselves on questions that are fundamentally about justice.
